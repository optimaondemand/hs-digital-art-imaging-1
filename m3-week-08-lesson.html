📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
Can you use everything you now know about type, on your own, to make a single page say exactly what you want it to say?
For three weeks you have studied type in pieces: hierarchy, pairing, expression, integration with an image. This week you put the pieces together. There is no new vocabulary today. This is a project week: you plan, build, and finish a poster or advertisement in Pixlr E that shows everything you have learned about letters as a design tool.

🔍 PART 3 · PLAN AND BUILD YOUR POSTER OR AD

Work through these steps in order. Rushing straight to Pixlr E without planning is the most common way a poster ends up cluttered.

Step 1: Choose your subject and message

Pick ONE of the following, or propose your own with your instructor's approval:

  • An advertisement for a product, event, or business (real or invented)
  • A poster promoting a cause, club, or performance
  • A movie or book poster for a real or invented title

Write your message in one sentence before you touch the software: what is the single most important thing a viewer should walk away knowing? That sentence becomes your headline.

Step 2: Sketch a thumbnail layout

On paper or in a notes app, draw 2 to 3 small boxes-and-lines sketches (a "thumbnail") showing roughly where your headline, subhead, expressive element, and body text will sit. Do not worry about exact wording yet; you are testing arrangement. Pick your favorite thumbnail before you move on.

Before you click: in your notebook or sketchbook, draw your thumbnail now. Then reveal what a finished plan should include.
A usable thumbnail names, even roughly: where the headline sits and how big it is relative to the page, where the subhead sits, where your one expressive touch goes, where the body text sits, and how much empty space (margin) surrounds everything. If your thumbnail has more than one expressive element, cut it back to one before you build.

Step 3: Choose your 2 to 3 paired fonts

Open Pixlr E at pixlr.com/e and select the Text tool. Type your headline, subhead, and body text as three separate text layers. Try at least two font pairings side by side in the font dropdown before you commit. Keep the pairing rule from Week 6: contrast enough to feel deliberate (for example, a bold display font for the headline with a plain, readable font for the body), never two fonts that look almost the same.

Step 4: Build your hierarchy

Set your three text layers to three clearly different sizes: headline largest, subhead in the middle, body text smallest but still readable (no smaller than 16 pixels on screen). Use the Layers panel to check that each layer is where you planned. Adjust color or weight if the reading order (first, second, last) is not obvious within two seconds of looking.

Step 5: Add your one expressive touch

Choose one place in your layout where the letters themselves become a visual element, not just words: a curved or tilted word, a headline that overlaps an image, a single letter enlarged and used as a shape, or type that follows the outline of an object. This is the compositional move Demuth made when he built an entire painting around the number 5. Keep it to one touch. A poster with an expressive move on every line has no hierarchy left.

Step 6: Check spacing, sign, and export

Look at the leading between lines and the tracking on your headline; tighten or loosen until the layout feels planned rather than accidental. Add your name and the date somewhere small and unobtrusive on the design (sign your work). Use File > Export to save as a PNG.

💡 PRE-FLIGHT CHECKLIST BEFORE YOU EXPORT
Walk through this list once before you export: One clear headline, largest on the page. One paired subhead font that contrasts with the headline. Body text left-aligned and at least 16 pixels. Exactly one expressive typographic touch, not several. Consistent spacing repeated across all text blocks. Your signature and date somewhere on the design. If any box would be unchecked, go back to that step before exporting.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI LAYOUT AND FONT GENERATORS
Some online tools can generate a full poster layout or suggest font pairings automatically from a text prompt. These tools do not know your specific message, audience, or which single expressive touch fits your subject. The planning decisions in Step 1 through Step 5, and the finished file you export, are what this project grades.
📝 VOCABULARY · MODULE RECAP
TypefaceThe overall letterform design or family, such as Georgia or Helvetica.
FontOne specific weight and style within a typeface.
Serif / Sans-serifTypefaces with, or without, small finishing strokes on the letters.
HierarchyThe order in which a viewer's eye is guided through text, built with size, weight, and color.
ReadabilityHow easily text can be read, based on size, spacing, and contrast.
AlignmentWhere text lines up relative to the page: left, centered, right, or justified.
Kerning / Tracking / LeadingSpace between two letters, space across a whole word or line, and vertical space between lines.
Rhythm (in type)Visual movement created by repeating consistent spacing and sizing across a layout.
Font pairingChoosing two or three fonts that contrast enough to feel deliberate, not accidental.
Expressive typeUsing letterforms as a visual or compositional element, not only as a message to read.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Four weeks ago, type was just words on a page. You learned that letters have anatomy and that size and spacing build hierarchy. You learned to pair fonts on purpose and to bend type for expression. You watched an artist build an entire painting around a single numeral. This week you proved you can do all of it yourself, without a worksheet walking you through each piece: plan a message, choose fonts that work together, build a reading order, and finish with one deliberate expressive move. That is the whole job of a designer, done in one page.
