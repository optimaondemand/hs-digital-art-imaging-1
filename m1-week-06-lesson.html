MODULE 1 · WEEK 6
History of Photography & Taking Digital Photographs
Lesson
📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How did photography go from a hours-long chemical process to an instant tap on a phone screen, and what stayed the same along the way?
The phone in your pocket can capture, edit, and share a photo in seconds. That took nearly two centuries to build. Knowing the path photography traveled helps you see your own camera, and your own photographs, differently.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Long before cameras existed, artists and scientists used a camera obscura: a dark box or room with a small hole that projected an image of the outside world onto a surface inside. The problem was permanence. The image could not be captured; it faded the moment the light changed.

📚 PART 2 · A TIMELINE OF PHOTOGRAPHY
1826 to 1827 A.D.
The First Permanent Photograph
French inventor Joseph Nicephore Niepce used a camera obscura and a pewter plate coated with a light-sensitive material called bitumen of Judea. After several hours of sunlight exposure, the hardened areas held an image while the rest washed away. The result, titled View from the Window at Le Gras, is considered the first photograph in history.
1839 A.D.
The Daguerreotype
Niepce's partner, Louis-Jacques-Mande Daguerre, improved the process using a polished, silver-coated copper plate made light-sensitive with iodine vapor, developed with mercury vapor, and fixed with a salt solution. Each daguerreotype was one of a kind since there were no negatives to make duplicates from. It became the first commercially successful photographic process, and studio portraits made photography both an art and a business.
1888 A.D.
The Kodak Revolution
George Eastman released the Kodak camera preloaded with film for 100 exposures. Customers mailed the whole camera back to Kodak, which developed the film, made prints, and reloaded it. Kodak's slogan, "You press the button, we do the rest," made photography accessible to everyday people instead of only trained professionals. Eastman's roll film also replaced heavy glass plates and later led to motion pictures.
Mid-1900s A.D.
The SLR Camera
SLR stands for Single-Lens Reflex. A mirror and prism system let photographers see through the viewfinder exactly what the lens saw; the mirror flipped up at the moment of exposure to let light reach the film. The first practical 35mm SLRs appeared in the 1950s and 1960s from companies like Nikon, Canon, and Pentax, offering interchangeable lenses and manual control. The SLR became the standard for professional and artistic photography, and its design later carried over into digital SLRs (DSLRs).
1975 A.D.
The First Digital Camera
Engineer Steven Sasson, working at Kodak, built the first digital camera. It weighed 8 pounds and recorded black-and-white images to a cassette tape, taking 23 seconds per photo. Kodak did not market it, fearing it would threaten film sales, but the idea did not disappear.
1990s to 2000s A.D.
Digital Cameras Take Over
Digital cameras became practical: memory cards replaced film, photos could be viewed instantly on a screen, and software like Adobe Photoshop opened new editing possibilities. By the 2000s, digital cameras had mostly replaced film cameras in everyday use.
2000 A.D. to Today
Smartphones and Computational Photography
In 2000, the Sharp J-SH04, the first phone with a built-in camera, was released in Japan. By the 2010s, smartphones carried high-resolution sensors and advanced lenses. Today's smartphones use artificial intelligence and computational photography to automatically adjust lighting, focus, and color, and instant sharing has turned photography into a tool for communication, self-expression, and social activism.
🔍 PART 3 · TAKING A GOOD DIGITAL PHOTOGRAPH

Every photographer in that timeline eventually had to make the same decision you make every time you raise a camera: what to include, and how. Being intentional means thinking ahead about the picture you want before you press the shutter, then using your technical skill to make it real. Not every photo needs this. Quick snapshots happen in the moment. But your best work will come from planning.

The single best way to improve is practice. The more you shoot, including the shots that do not work, the faster you learn what does. Mistakes teach you exactly as much as successes do.

Orientation is the direction you hold your camera or device, and it changes how your photo is displayed:

Landscape orientation

The photo is wider than it is tall. Good for scenery, groups, and anything that spreads out horizontally.

Portrait orientation

The photo is taller than it is wide. Good for a single upright subject, like a person or a tall object.

Landscape: wider than tall
Portrait: taller than wide

Left: Ansel Adams, The Tetons and the Snake River, 1942 (National Park Service, public domain, source: Wikimedia Commons). Right: Julia Margaret Cameron (British, 1815–1879), Julia Jackson, 1867, albumen silver print, Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain (source: metmuseum.org).

Beyond orientation, composition, how all the visual elements are arranged, decides whether a photo reads as art. That means paying attention to everything in the frame: placement, colors, shapes, lines, and the space around your subject. Two techniques help you control what the viewer notices:

Inclusion

Purposely keeping certain background elements in the frame because they add visual interest or tell part of the story. You can practice this by placing your subject in a setting that adds context, like outdoors, or by shifting your own position to find a background that enhances the image.

Exclusion

Removing or avoiding anything that distracts from your subject: clearing clutter from the background, changing your orientation, or moving to a simpler location. Some photographers blur the background using depth of field to eliminate distractions while keeping the subject sharp.

Exclusion: a plain background isolates the subject

💡 A QUICK PRE-SHOT CHECKLIST
Before you tap the shutter: Decide your subject. Choose landscape or portrait orientation on purpose. Scan the whole frame, not just the subject, and decide what you are including or excluding. Then shoot more than one version.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · COMPUTATIONAL PHOTOGRAPHY
Modern smartphones use AI to blend multiple exposures, sharpen faces, and adjust color automatically the moment you take a photo. This is useful, but it works after you have already decided where to stand, how to orient the camera, and what to include or exclude. Those framing decisions are yours, and they are what separates an intentional photograph from a lucky one.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Photography took nearly two hundred years to go from a fading projection in a dark box to an instant image on a screen, but the goal never changed: capturing a moment on purpose. Every camera in that history, from a pewter plate exposed for hours to the phone in your pocket, asked its user the same question: what do you want to remember, and how do you want it framed?
