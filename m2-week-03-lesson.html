📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
What decisions turn ordinary light into a photograph with a mood?
You already know that where light comes from changes a photo. This week goes one layer deeper: what kind of light it is, how much of it reaches your camera, and what temperature it carries. Small changes here (a cloud passing, a lamp switched on, a slightly longer exposure) can turn the same scene calm, dramatic, warm, or cold.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

The same subject, exposed to more or less light, produces very different photos. Exposure is how much light reaches your camera's sensor. Too little light and a photo is underexposed (too dark, shadows swallow detail). Too much light and a photo is overexposed (too bright, highlights wash out detail). Between the two sits a balanced exposure where both shadow and highlight detail are visible.

☀️ PART 2 · NATURAL VS. ARTIFICIAL LIGHT

Natural light

Light from the sun or sky. It changes constantly through the day, from harsh midday sun to the soft glow of golden hour, and shifts with weather: clouds, rain, fog, and snow all change how natural light behaves.

Artificial light

Light from a human-made source: a lamp, a phone flashlight, a streetlight, a stage light. Artificial light is more predictable and controllable than natural light, but it often carries a strong color cast of its own.

Whichever kind of light you use, you are always managing exposure, the amount of light that reaches your camera. On a phone or basic camera, exposure is often controlled automatically, but you can influence it: move your subject out of direct sun, add or remove a light source, or use your device's exposure/brightness slider before you shoot. Getting exposure right protects two things at once: shadow detail (the darkest parts of your photo still show some texture and form) and highlight detail (the brightest parts are not a pure white blank).

💡 CHECK YOUR SHADOWS AND HIGHLIGHTS
Before you shoot, glance at the darkest and brightest parts of your scene. If the darkest area already looks like solid black or the brightest area looks like solid white on your screen, you are likely to lose detail there. Reposition your subject or adjust your exposure setting before you press the shutter, since it is far easier to fix at capture than to recover lost detail afterward.
🌈 PART 3 · COLOR TEMPERATURE AND MOOD

Light is not just bright or dim; it also carries color temperature, a warm-to-cool range that changes the feel of a photo before you touch any editing tool. Warm light leans orange and gold. Cool light leans blue.

Warm (candlelight, golden hour, indoor lamps)Neutral (daylight)Cool (shade, overcast, blue hour)

Color temperature and mood travel together. A candlelit room or a golden hour photo feels warm, intimate, or nostalgic. An overcast day or a blue hour photo feels cool, calm, or somber. Indoor artificial lamps often push warm/orange; a shaded area on a sunny day often pushes cool/blue, which is why a photo taken partly in sun and partly in shade can look like it has two different color casts unless you correct it.

Before you look: if you wanted a photo to feel calm and a little melancholy, would you choose warm or cool light? Decide your answer, then click to check.
Cool light. Blue-leaning color temperature, like an overcast day or blue hour, generally reads as calm, quiet, or somber, while warm light tends to read as cozy or energetic instead.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI IMAGE TOOLS
An AI image generator can produce an image described as "warm golden hour light" or "moody blue exposure" from a text prompt. It has not read an actual light meter, walked into an actual room, or decided how to protect shadow and highlight detail in a real scene. This week's project is graded on the exposure and color temperature decisions you make with your own camera.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Light is the raw material of every photograph, and it has three dials you can learn to read: source, exposure, and temperature. A photographer who watches all three before pressing the shutter is not relying on luck. They are deciding, in advance, what mood the finished photo will carry.
