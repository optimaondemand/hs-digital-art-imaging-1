📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How can an artist correct a photo's exposure and composition after the shutter has already closed, without destroying the original?
For the past four weeks you have worked on getting the shot right in camera: composition, light, and exposure. Even a well-planned photo sometimes comes out darker or lighter than you wanted. This week you stop shooting and start editing. You will learn the habits that keep your original photo safe, then use Pixlr E's Crop tool and exposure tools to rescue an underexposed image.

📚 PART 2 · BEFORE YOU EDIT: NON-DESTRUCTIVE HABITS

Editing lets you adjust, improve, and experiment with an image after it has been taken. Small changes can have a big impact, but only if you edit thoughtfully and protect your original along the way. Build these four habits before you touch a single tool.

  • Keep your original safe. In Pixlr E, use Layer > Duplicate Layer before you adjust anything. Edit the duplicate, not the bottom layer.
  • Don't overedit. Pushing a tool too far makes an image look unnatural: blown-out highlights, crushed shadows, or colors that no longer look real.
  • Work in layers. Give each adjustment its own layer in the Layers panel so you can compare, tweak, or remove any single change without redoing the rest.
  • Export with a new file name. When you finish, use File > Export and save the result under a new name so your original photo file is never overwritten.
💡 NON-DESTRUCTIVE ADJUSTMENT LAYERS
In Pixlr E, an adjustment applied to a duplicated layer sits on top of your original pixels instead of replacing them. The layer below stays untouched in the Layers panel the whole time. That means every edit is reversible: you can hide a layer, lower its opacity, or delete it and the original image underneath is still there, exactly as you shot it.

Step one: start with a strong original

Begin with a photo you are proud of. Even a photo that already follows the rule of thirds usually has room to improve its composition. That is where the Crop tool comes in, one of the simplest and most powerful tools in Pixlr E.

🔍 PART 3 · CROP AND CORRECT EXPOSURE IN PIXLR E

The Crop tool

Find Crop in the left toolbar. It lets you cut out parts of the image you do not want and change the balance and focus of your composition. Pixlr E's Crop tool includes:

  • Built-in aspect ratios (such as 1:1, 4:5, or 16:9) for standard sizes.
  • A straighten control if the horizon or a vertical line in your photo is slightly tilted.
  • A draggable rectangle over your image; pull the edges or corners to keep only what strengthens your subject.

When you are happy with the crop, click Apply. Try more than one aspect ratio before you commit; a square crop and a wide crop can highlight a subject very differently.

Correcting exposure

Even with strong technique, some photos still need help after the shot. Find these tools under Pixlr E's Adjustment menu. Duplicate your layer first, then try each tool on the copy.

Brightness & Contrast

Brightness adjusts the highlights, the lightest areas of the image. Contrast adjusts the difference between the darkest and lightest parts. Raising brightness lifts dark areas; adjusting contrast fixes an image that looks too flat or too harsh.

Exposure

Exposure changes the overall light across the whole image at once. If a photo is underexposed, raising Exposure is often the fastest way to bring details back into view.

Shadows & Highlights

Unlike Exposure, this tool targets only the dark or light pixels instead of brightening everything. Increase Shadows to bring hidden detail out of dark areas. Reduce Highlights to recover detail in bright areas that look washed out. This is the most precise of the three tools.

Picture a photo that is slightly underexposed: the colors underneath look rich, but the overall image is too dark. Here is one order to try in Pixlr E:

  1. Open the photo in Pixlr E and duplicate the layer.
  2. On the duplicate, open Adjustment > Exposure and raise it until details become visible.
  3. Open Adjustment > Shadows & Highlights and increase Shadows to pull detail out of the darkest corners without touching the rest of the image.
  4. Finish with Adjustment > Brightness & Contrast to fine-tune, since a big exposure jump can flatten contrast.
  5. Compare the adjusted layer against the original layer in the Layers panel. Keep only what improves the photo.

Saving your edited image

Before you export, check your work: Are the details clearer? Did the crop balance the composition? Compare the edited layer against the original layer, still sitting safely underneath. Decide whether the adjustments improved the photo or whether the shot needs to be retaken next time; both are useful conclusions. When you are satisfied, export as a PNG or JPEG under a new file name so both versions exist.

Before you look: in your notebook, write down which of the three exposure tools (Brightness & Contrast, Exposure, Shadows & Highlights) you would try first on a photo that is dark everywhere except one overexposed bright window. Then click to compare.
A bright, blown-out window next to dark surroundings is exactly the case Shadows & Highlights is built for: reduce Highlights to pull detail back into the window without darkening the rest of the room. Exposure would darken the whole image, including the parts that already look fine.
🎨 STUDIO HABIT: EDIT IN PASSES, NOT ALL AT ONCE
Professional editors rarely fix everything with one slider. They duplicate a layer, adjust exposure, check it, duplicate again, adjust shadows and highlights, check it, then crop last so they are judging the final composition with corrected light. Working this way, one adjustment at a time on its own layer, is what makes an edit reversible and keeps you from overediting without noticing.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AUTO-ENHANCE AND ONE-CLICK FILTERS
Many photo apps include an AI "auto-enhance" or one-tap filter that adjusts exposure, contrast, and color all at once. It can be a fast starting point, but it makes every decision for you in a single step and gives you no separate control over Exposure versus Shadows & Highlights versus Brightness & Contrast. This week's assignment asks you to make each adjustment yourself, tool by tool, so you can see and control exactly what each one changes. That tool-by-tool judgment is what is graded.
📝 VOCABULARY
Non-Destructive EditingEditing a duplicate or separate layer so the original pixels stay intact and every change can be undone.
UnderexposedA photo that received too little light, so it looks too dark overall even though its colors are intact.
Crop ToolThe tool used to cut out unwanted parts of an image and change its composition or aspect ratio.
Aspect RatioThe proportion between an image's width and height, such as 1:1 or 16:9.
Brightness & ContrastAdjustment tools controlling highlight level (brightness) and the range between darkest and lightest areas (contrast).
Exposure (adjustment)An editing tool that raises or lowers the overall light level across the entire image.
Shadows & HighlightsAn adjustment tool that targets only dark pixels (shadows) or only light pixels (highlights), rather than the whole image.
Duplicate LayerA copy of an image layer in the Layers panel, used so edits can be made without touching the original.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A photo that comes out too dark is not a finished decision, it is a starting point. Non-destructive editing means you can duplicate, adjust, compare, and undo without ever risking the original shot. This week you learned three ways to correct exposure and one way to recompose with the Crop tool. The habit that ties them together matters more than any single slider: protect your original, work in layers, and export under a new name.
