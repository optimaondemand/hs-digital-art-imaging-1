📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How does technology influence the way artists create, share, and experience art in the digital age?
When you think of digital art, what comes to mind? Maybe a video game, a filter on your phone, or a design you scrolled past this morning. Every one of those was made by an artist making choices. This year you will learn to make those choices on purpose, and this week you begin by learning what "digital art" actually means.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT DIGITAL ART IS

Digital art is any artwork that uses digital technology at any stage of the creative process. That could mean:

  • Drawing directly on a tablet or computer
  • Scanning a hand-drawn sketch and editing it on screen
  • Building an image completely from software

When an artwork is meant to be viewed on a screen, website, or app, it is called digital imaging. Artists have been mixing art and technology for decades. Today it is hard to find art that has not been touched by technology, whether it is a digital photo of a painting or a virtual museum tour.

Almost every digital image is one of two kinds. Knowing which one you are working in changes how you edit it.

Raster (pixel) images

Made of a fixed grid of pixels. Photographs and most painted digital art are raster. They look great at their true size but turn blocky if you enlarge them too far. Pixlr E, which you will use in this course, is a raster editor.

Vector images

Made of math-defined lines and shapes instead of pixels. Logos and clean graphics are often vector because they stay crisp at any size, from a business card to a billboard.

🔍 PART 3 · HOW TECHNOLOGY AND ART WORK TOGETHER

Technology has changed art in four big ways. As you read, notice that in every case a human artist is still making the decisions. The tools are new; the thinking is the same craft artists have practiced for centuries.

1. Preserving art

Museums scan and photograph fragile paintings to detect damage early and plan restorations. Digital records also let you study a masterpiece up close from anywhere. The image below is a public-domain scan released by the Art Institute of Chicago.

2. Pushing creative boundaries

Some artists build their work around a new technology. Andy Warhol used screen printing, a process that begins with a photograph, to repeat the same image in different colors and explore fame and mass production. You can view his Marilyn Diptych on the museum's own site: Tate · Warhol, Marilyn Diptych.

Warhol was not the first artist to build a career on a repeatable printing process. Decades earlier, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec used lithography, printing from a flat stone or plate, to produce the same poster image by the thousands for the streets of Paris.

Lithography let Toulouse-Lautrec print the same striking image over and over, the same repeatable-imagery idea Warhol later pushed further with screen printing.

3. Creating immersive experiences

Technology also changes how we experience art. The collective teamLab builds room-sized digital installations you can walk through, where projected flowers bloom and fade around you. Explore their work here: teamlab.art. Later this year you will make and share art inside a virtual space of your own.

4. Art in the digital world

Digital art can be edited, erased, and redone endlessly, which frees artists to take risks and experiment. Because a file can be copied and shared instantly, questions of ownership and permission matter more than ever, which leads to the guidelines below.

🎨 BEING A RESPONSIBLE DIGITAL ARTIST
Use your own images. If practice photos are provided, use them; otherwise take your own. Be original. Your work does not have to be perfect, but it should be yours. Avoid plagiarism. Copying someone else's artwork or photo and presenting it as your own, even with small edits, is plagiarism; if an idea or image is not yours, say where it came from. Think before you post. Once art is online it can stay there, and that includes comments: keep critiques and replies to classmates specific and respectful, the way you would want to be treated. Check your sources. Before you use a fact or image you find online, confirm it comes from a museum, a real artist's own site, or another source you trust, not just the first result in a search. Respect other artists and their privacy: do not use or share images of people or others' work without permission. Sign and date your art to show it is yours.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI IMAGE TOOLS
AI image generators can produce a picture from a text prompt in seconds and can be useful for gathering ideas. They cannot make the compositional decisions this course is teaching you: what to include, how to arrange it, where the light falls, which colors carry the mood. Those decisions, and the work you produce with your own tools, are what is graded here.
📝 VOCABULARY
Digital ArtAny artwork that uses digital technology at some stage of creation.
Digital ImagingArtwork made to be viewed on a screen, website, or app.
PixelOne of the tiny colored squares that make up a screen image.
ResolutionThe number of pixels in an image; more pixels mean more detail.
Raster ImageAn image built from a fixed grid of pixels (photos, digital paintings).
Vector ImageAn image built from math-defined lines and shapes; stays crisp at any size.
Screen PrintingA process, starting from a photo or stencil, that presses ink through a mesh screen.
CompositionHow the parts of an artwork are arranged.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Digital art is not a shortcut; it is a new set of tools for the same old goal, saying something with an image. Technology preserves art, pushes it further, surrounds us with it, and spreads it instantly. Your job this year is to be the artist behind the tool: the one making the choices. This week, you make your first ones.
