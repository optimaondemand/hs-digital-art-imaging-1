📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How do artists use letters themselves, not just words, as a visual design tool?
You read hundreds of pieces of type every day: signs, menus, app icons, the title of this lesson. Most of it you never notice, because good type design gets out of the way. This week you start noticing on purpose. You are moving from images built out of pixels to images built out of letters, and letters have their own design rules.
👀 PART 1 · LOOK FIRST

Every letterform is built from parts with names, the same way a body has an anatomy. Knowing the parts lets you talk precisely about why one typeface feels different from another. Look at the real, physical letters below, the way printers set text by hand before computers existed.

Every one of these physical letters, like every letter on this page, is built from parts with names. The x-height is the height of a lowercase "x" and most other lowercase letters; the baseline is the line every letter sits on; a descender drops below the baseline (as in g, j, p, q, y); a counter is a fully or partly enclosed space inside a letter.

📚 PART 2 · TYPEFACE, FONT, AND SERIF VS. SANS-SERIF

People use "font" and "typeface" as if they mean the same thing, but they do not. A typeface is the overall design, the family, such as Georgia or Helvetica. A font is one specific weight and style within that family, such as Georgia Bold Italic at 24 point. One typeface can contain dozens of fonts.

Almost every typeface belongs to one of two broad groups.

Serif

Reading is believing.

Has small finishing strokes ("feet") at the ends of letters. Traditional, associated with print, often used for long passages of body text because the serifs help guide the eye along a line.

Sans-serif

Reading is believing.

"Sans" means without: no feet on the letters. Clean and modern, common in screen interfaces, signage, and headlines because it stays legible at very small or very large sizes.

🔍 PART 3 · HIERARCHY, ALIGNMENT, AND SPACING

Hierarchy is the order in which a viewer's eye is meant to read the text on a page: what they see first, second, and last. Designers build hierarchy with size, weight, and color, not with words alone. Look at this simple layout.

Readability is how easily text can be read at all. It is affected by size, spacing, color contrast against the background, and how "busy" the typeface is. A gorgeous typeface that nobody can actually read has failed at its job.

Alignment is where text lines up relative to the page or text box.

LEFT-ALIGNED
Every line starts at the same edge. The most common choice for body text because the eye always returns to the same starting point.
CENTERED
Each line is balanced around a middle point. Common on invitations and short headlines, harder to read in long passages.
RIGHT-ALIGNED
Every line ends at the same edge. Useful for captions or labels placed beside an image on the left.
JUSTIFIED
Text stretches to fill the full width on both edges, like a newspaper column, which can create uneven gaps between words.

🎵 PART 4 · RHYTHM IN TYPE

You may already know rhythm as a Principle of Design: the repeat of an element that creates visual movement, the same way a beat repeats in music. In typography, rhythm comes from consistent spacing, sizing, and alignment repeated across a whole layout. When every headline in a document is the same size and every paragraph has the same leading, the page develops a steady visual "beat" that makes it feel organized before a reader has read a single word.

Breaking that rhythm on purpose, one oversized word, one line set apart, is how designers create emphasis. Breaking it by accident, uneven spacing scattered around a page, is what makes a layout feel unplanned. This week's exercise asks you to build steady rhythm first, before you learn to break it on purpose in Week 6.

Before you click: in your notebook, sketch three boxes stacked (headline, subheading, body) and guess which font size you would use for each. Then reveal a working example.
A common working ratio: headline at roughly 2.5 to 3 times the body text size, subheading at roughly 1.3 to 1.5 times the body text size, and body text no smaller than 16 pixels on screen. These are starting ratios to adjust by eye, not fixed rules.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI FONT AND LAYOUT SUGGESTIONS
Some design tools use AI to suggest font pairings or auto-arrange text on a layout. These suggestions can be a fast starting point, but they do not know your specific message, audience, or the hierarchy you are trying to build. Deciding what your viewer should read first, second, and last is a judgment call this lesson is teaching you to make yourself.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

Letters are not just a way to record words; they are shapes with their own design rules, the same as any other element of art. Hierarchy tells a viewer where to look first, spacing tells the eye how fast to move, and rhythm holds a whole layout together. This week you learned to see those rules. Next week you learn how to bend them for expression.
