📖 ESSENTIAL QUESTION
How do you bring photography and editing together into one finished body of work?
You have spent this semester learning to see light and to shape an image after you capture it. This week you do not learn a new tool. You plan the project that pulls everything together: a three-photo series, lit with intention and edited with intention, introduced by your own words. This is the halfway point of the year, and this project is your proof of everything you have learned so far.

📚 PART 2 · THE SEMESTER SO FAR

This capstone draws on everything you have built since Week 1. Here is the short version.

Module 1: Digital Imaging Foundations

You learned what digital art is, practiced the elements and principles of design, worked with color and shape tools, and moved into photography: line and space in a frame, a short look at photography's history, how to take an intentional photograph, and where to find inspiration.

Module 2 so far: Capturing Light & Editing

You studied lighting techniques (side, back, diffused, and more) and built a three-photo series unified by a theme. Then you moved into editing in Pixlr E: correcting exposure with Brightness/Contrast, Exposure, and Shadows/Highlights; adjusting color with Hue/Saturation, Color Balance, and Vibrance; and using filters to direct attention. Every one of those skills is a tool you now choose from, rather than a step you follow in order.

💡 WHY THIS WEEK HAS NO NEW TOOL
This week you do not learn anything new. You plan how to use what you already know. Planning before you shoot and edit is itself part of the craft: a photographer who plans a shot list gets better results than one who starts shooting and hopes for the best.
🎨 PART 3 · THE CAPSTONE PROJECT

Your End-of-Semester Project is an edited photo series. It has three parts: the photos, the edits, and the artist statement.

1. Three original photos, one theme

  • Take three original digital photos of your own. Use any camera or device that takes digital photos.
  • Unify them with one theme: subject-based, a compositional technique, movement, texture, or color. Pick the theme approach that felt strongest in your Week 5 series, or try a new one.
  • Use at least two different lighting techniques across the three photos (for example, side lighting on one and backlighting on another).
  • At least one photo must show texture clearly.

2. Edit each photo in Pixlr E

  • On at least one photo, apply a crop and/or an exposure correction (Brightness/Contrast, Exposure, or Shadows/Highlights).
  • On at least one photo, apply a color adjustment (Hue/Saturation, Color Balance, or Vibrance) or a filter.
  • A single photo can carry more than one edit. Every photo should show a deliberate improvement over the original, not an overedited or unnatural result.

3. A short artist statement

Type a statement of 100 to 150 words in Word. In it, explain your theme, the lighting choices you made and why, and the specific editing decisions you made in Pixlr E on each photo. Save it as a Word document in your portfolio folder.

💡 ALWAYS KEEP YOUR ORIGINALS
Before you edit, duplicate each photo so your original is untouched. Save each edited version under a new file name. This way you always have the "before" to compare against the "after," and you can start over if an edit does not work out.
📋 PART 4 · PLAN BEFORE YOU SHOOT

Work through these steps in order this week. Each one sets up the next.

Choose your theme

Decide what will unify your three photos: subject, composition, movement, texture, or color. Write it in one sentence you could say out loud to someone looking at your series.

Build a shot list

For each of the three photos, note the subject, the location, and which lighting technique you plan to use there. Make sure at least two different techniques appear across the list, and mark which photo will show texture.

Make an editing plan

Before you shoot, decide roughly how you expect to edit each photo: will one need a crop or exposure fix, will another need a color adjustment or filter? You can adjust this plan once you see your actual photos, but planning now helps you shoot with editing in mind.

Shoot, then edit

Take your three photos following your shot list. Open each one in Pixlr E, duplicate the layer, and apply your planned edits. Compare each edited version to its original before you export.

Write your artist statement

With your finished photos in front of you, write your 100 to 150 word statement: name your theme, explain your lighting choices, and describe your editing decisions on each photo.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI IMAGE GENERATORS VS. YOUR OWN PHOTOGRAPHS
AI image generators can produce a picture of almost anything from a text prompt, but they do not take a photograph of the real world and they cannot capture the specific light, texture, or subject in front of you at a specific moment. This project is graded on your own original photography and your own editing decisions in Pixlr E. A generated image cannot substitute for any of the three required photos.
📝 VOCABULARY
Photo SeriesA collection of images that share a common theme, idea, or visual style.
ThemeThe idea or quality that connects the photos in a series, such as subject, composition, movement, texture, or color.
CohesionThe sense that separate images belong together as one body of work.
Shot ListA written plan of what to photograph, where, and how, made before shooting begins.
Artist StatementA short written explanation of an artwork's theme, choices, and process, written by the artist.
Lighting TechniqueA specific way of using light on a subject, such as side lighting, backlighting, or diffused lighting.
TextureThe surface quality of a subject, such as rough, smooth, soft, or patterned, made visible through light.
Capstone ProjectA culminating project that brings together skills learned across a unit or semester.

💪 THE BIG IDEA

A photographer who plans is not less creative than one who improvises; planning is what lets creativity survive contact with a real camera, real light, and a real deadline. This week you connect two halves of your semester, seeing light and shaping it afterward, into one body of work you chose and can explain in your own words. That is the whole point of a capstone: not a new skill, but proof of the skills you already own.
